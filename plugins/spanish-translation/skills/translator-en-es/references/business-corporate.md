# Business and corporate translation

This is the middle ground: less rigid than legal, less constrained than UI strings. The risk is sounding stiff, robotic, or off-tone. A masterful business translation reads like it was *written* by a senior bilingual professional, not translated.

## The formal-you decision

The single highest-leverage choice in business Spanish is `tú` vs `usted`. Get this right and the rest follows.

### Default to *usted* (singular) and *ustedes* (plural) when:

- Writing to a customer you don't have a peer relationship with.
- Writing to a prospect, executive, vendor, or partner.
- The English source uses formal markers (`Dear Mr. Smith`, `Sincerely`, `We would like to invite you`, `kindly`, `please find attached`).
- The audience is enterprise B2B, financial services, legal, healthcare, government.
- You're unsure. *Usted* is almost never wrong in a professional context; *tú* used wrong sounds presumptuous.

### Use *tú* when:

- The source is clearly casual (peer-to-peer email, internal team chat, consumer marketing aimed at younger or casual audiences).
- The product or brand voice is explicitly informal (consumer app, lifestyle brand, startup marketing).
- The user tells you to.

### Never mix within a single document.

If a marketing email opens with `tú` it ends with `tú`. If a contract footer is `usted` then the call-to-action button in that same email is `usted`. Inconsistency reads as careless.

## Email register patterns

| English source | Formal ES (usted) | Warm ES (tú) |
|---|---|---|
| Dear Mr./Ms. <Last> | Estimado/a Sr./Sra. <Apellido> | Hola <First> |
| Dear <First> | Estimado/a <First> | Hola <First> |
| Hi <First> | Estimado/a <First> | Hola <First> / Hola, <First> |
| I hope this finds you well | Espero que se encuentre bien | Espero que estés bien |
| I'm writing to... | Le escribo para... | Te escribo para... |
| Please let me know | Por favor, indíqueme / Quedo atento a su respuesta | Avísame / Cuéntame |
| Looking forward to hearing from you | Quedo a la espera de su respuesta | Espero tu respuesta |
| Best regards | Atentamente / Cordialmente | Saludos / Un saludo |
| Kind regards | Saludos cordiales | Saludos |
| Sincerely | Atentamente | (don't use formal closings with tú) |
| Thanks | Gracias | Gracias / Mil gracias |
| Many thanks | Muchas gracias / Le agradezco | Muchas gracias |

Don't translate the structure too literally. English emails open and close with more ceremony than Spanish business emails. A `Dear Mr. Smith, I hope this email finds you well. I'm writing to inform you that...` can compress to `Estimado Sr. Smith: Le escribo para informarle que...` without losing register. The Spanish reader expects the opener to be efficient.

Note the **colon after the salutation** in Spanish formal letters: `Estimado Sr. Smith:` (not comma).

## Common business calques to kill

| Don't write | Write instead |
|---|---|
| Es importante para nosotros que... | Nos importa que... / Es fundamental que... |
| Hacer seguro que... | Asegurarse de que... / Garantizar que... |
| Tomar ventaja de | Aprovechar |
| En orden de | Para / A fin de |
| En adición a | Además de |
| Basado en | Con base en (LatAm) / A partir de |
| Manejar (a person, a team) | Dirigir, gestionar, liderar |
| Aplicar (a un trabajo) | Postularse a / Solicitar |
| Llamar de vuelta | Devolver la llamada |
| Eventualmente | Con el tiempo / Finalmente |
| Reportar a (un jefe) | OK in business Spanish, but `depender de` or `responder ante` are more native |
| Eficiente y efectivo | One or the other, not both — `eficaz` covers what you usually mean |
| 24/7 | Las 24 horas, los 7 días de la semana (or keep "24/7" in informal contexts) |

## Marketing copy

Marketing translation is the hardest because the goal isn't faithfulness — it's **equivalent persuasion**. A literal translation of a slogan often falls flat. Permission and expectations:

- **Headlines and slogans**: deliver an equivalent in the target language. If the source uses a pun, find a pun. If the source uses rhythm or alliteration, find rhythm or alliteration. Document the adaptation in the notes — flag that this is *transcreation*, not literal translation, and offer the literal version too.
- **Body copy**: closer to literal, but still natural. Don't preserve English sentence structure — Spanish marketing prose tolerates longer sentences and more rhetorical flourish than English; lean in moderately, don't go baroque.
- **CTAs**: short, imperative, action-oriented. Match the English brevity even if Spanish wants to expand.
- **Brand names and product names**: never translate unless the brand has an official localized name.

When you transcreate (rather than translate), **say so in the notes**:

> Note: The headline "Move fast. Break nothing." was adapted as "Avance rápido. Sin riesgos." rather than translated literally, to preserve the punchy two-clause rhythm. Literal translation: "Muévase rápido. No rompa nada." — flat and odd in Spanish. Let me know if you prefer the literal version.

## Proposals, reports, and internal docs

- Use formal register by default.
- Tables, headings, numbered sections: preserve structure exactly.
- Acronyms: keep the source acronym, gloss on first use. "KPI" → `KPI (indicador clave de desempeño)` on first use, then `KPI` thereafter. Some acronyms are bilingual and stay (KPI, ROI, SLA, NDA). Others have established Spanish equivalents (CEO → director general, but in modern LatAm business `CEO` is widely accepted and often preferred).
- Numbers: Spanish uses comma as decimal separator and period (or space) as thousands separator in most LatAm countries — except Mexico, which often follows the US convention. **Default for neutral LatAm: comma decimal, period thousands** (`1.234,56`). If translating for a US-Mexico business audience, mirror the source convention and flag the choice in the notes.
- Currency: keep currency symbols and amounts as written. `$1,000 USD` stays `$1,000 USD` (or `USD 1.000` in some conventions — flag if you change format).

## What to flag in translator's notes (business)

- The formality decision (tú vs usted) and why.
- Any acronym you glossed or kept untranslated.
- Marketing transcreations — always show the literal version too.
- Number/currency format if you changed conventions.
- Job titles where the LatAm convention differs from US English (e.g., "VP of Engineering" can stay as VP de Ingeniería or `Vicepresidente de Ingeniería` — the former is more common in modern tech, the latter in traditional corporate). Pick one, flag the choice.
- Cultural references that don't carry over (a US-specific holiday, a sports analogy) — note that you adapted or removed.
